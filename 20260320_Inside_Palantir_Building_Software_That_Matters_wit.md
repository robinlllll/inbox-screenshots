# Summary

America's will to compete, particularly in AI and defense, is at risk due to internal issues. Shyam Sankar, CTO at Palantir Technologies, alongside Katherine Boyle, addresses how America can reclaim its competitive edge by inspiring "latent heretics" and re-engaging its industrial base. Sankar argues that America's focus should be on empowering the American worker with AI, drawing parallels to historical figures like Hyman Rickover and highlighting the need for leadership that protects and encourages innovation. He stresses the importance of co-locating R&D with production, and the need to mobilize the whole country, not just the Department of War, to prevent larger conflicts. Sankar also touches on the role of storytelling and film in shaping national will and inspiring future generations.

# Takeaways

* America's biggest risk is internal decay—a loss of national will and focus—rather than external threats.
* The American industrial base that won World War II was characterized by the integration of commercial and defense sectors, where everyday consumer products subsidized national security.
* Competition inside the government services, rather than among defense contractors, has historically driven defense innovation.
* The consolidation of defense contractors after the Cold War led to conformity and financialization, expunging the heretical thinkers needed for innovation.
* AI presents an opportunity to fix the breakdown between wage growth and GDP growth by empowering American workers with superpowers, underwriting reindustrialization.
* The value in AI will accrue at the chips layer and the AI infrastructure layer, while models are being commoditized.
* The co-location of R&D and production is critical for innovation, as making the thing enables innovation in how the thing is made and what the thing is.
* The goal of applying AI should be to enhance human capabilities and achieve dominance, rather than simply replacing people.
* Storytelling through movies and entertainment is a powerful tool for shaping national will and inspiring future generations to believe in American greatness.
* Leadership within institutions is essential to protect and empower heretics, who are crucial for driving innovation and change.

# Q & A

**Q: What was it about 18 months ago where everyone seems to agree on the thing that was so contrarian for many years, where you were sort of banging your head against a wall saying this needs to happen?**

A: It all comes down to leadership. We call them the founding fathers for a reason. There is something special about the American spirit. Every founding story is equal parts heresy and heroism. We had the right people who saw, hey, this is not working, and the shot clock is running out, and we have to do something. We had those people both inside the building and outside the building. So it's kind of a conspiracy coalition of the willing, coalition of the capable to go do that. The election is a big part of it, not to make it political, but just being able to get in leadership that viewed it with clarity and set the conditions to make this change happen.

---

**Q: Why did you decide now's the time to write the canonical book of what needs to happen in America, join the Army, and also be leading Palantir?**

A: There's a coded thread through that, which is like, how do we mobilize to prevent a bigger conflict? If you're really paying attention, it's hard not to think that we're kind of in the late 30s here, that things are brewing. We're coming out of the malaise of having won the Cold War, or the Soviets having lost it, perhaps more accurately. That led to a lot of bad behaviors that allowed us to believe a lot of lies about the future that we're now marking to market. We will not have the luxury that we really had in World War II of letting the adversary attack us first and then deciding to mobilize. Leadership from FDR realized in the late 30s that we needed to mobilize, but there was not yet a national will or popular mandate to do so. Lend-Lease provided the mechanism to do that. It took us 18 months to build factories and retool them, and we were able to create capability deterrence that we sold to the Brits and to the Soviets, such that when World War II really kicked off for us, when Pearl Harbor happened, we were at full rate production.

---

**Q: Who are the most exciting heroes in the defense industry in tech? Like who's the person you look at and you say, gosh, that is the person that people don't know about, they need to know.**

A: It's hard to pick only one. Some of these figures are historical figures, Monarch Shriver, Edward Hall, Hyman Rickover, James Boyd, John Boyd, sorry. But the one that's new is Colonel Drew Cukor. Cukor is the father of Maven. Here you have this Marine Colonel, born, raised by a single mother, Southern California, very modest background. His only way of going to college was ROTC. Joined the Marines. Lots of incredible experiences there, but he had a seminal experience where he was trying to evacuate Yazidi refugees who were fleeing ISIS. A young Marine looking at ISR made a call that he thought he saw RPGs, and that would have made it unsafe for the Marines to land and exfil these people. And there actually wasn't one. As a consequence, you have order of thousands of people who were tortured, enslaved and raped because of this failure of operation, and it just changed this man. When he had an opportunity in the basement of the Pentagon on a project with no resources to go after bringing AI to the department, he leaned all the way in. Everyone hated him. Everyone tried to kill him. Every service thought they were doing AI. People tried to throw IG investigations at him. One of the details we document is that someone said that Colonel Kukor is housing Iranians in his basement. So they actually sent out criminal investigators to his house to look at this. Here's a devout Mormon, four daughters, 1400 square foot home that doesn't have a basement, by the way. The investigators were just completely dumbfounded.

**Q: Being on the inside now, what surprised you about the Army?**

A: What's been hugely impressive to me is the quality of talent in our green suitors. People who are not formally trained computer scientists, people who have just learned these things. The most compelling AI applications I'm seeing across commercial or private sector or public sector are being built by these green suitors. There's something about the existential stakes. You're not doing this for fun. You're not doing this for 10% efficiency. It's a binary outcome win or lose. The other thing about this moment that I think is really interesting with AI is it's massively empowering to people with specific skills. It is the Intel Warrant Officer who really knows their domain. 10 years ago, what would they have done though with their idea? Make a PowerPoint slide? Brief some program bureaucrat who would tell them how bad their idea is? Now they spend two weeks, they build it themselves. Now they're having an empirical conversation about how what they've built actually drives the army forward and everyone is quick to adopt it because everyone wants to win.

---

**Q: How do you make sense of the SaaS products?**

A: Both things are true. I would give you a different rubric to think about it, which is what software is really fundamentally about beta and what software is about alpha? The software that's about beta is going to really struggle. This is software that made you more similar to everyone else. This has been my historical critique of the software industrial complex, which is that the feedback loop for the people building the software is, can I sell it? Not, did it add value? The advent of AI allows you to make software that's specific to you. It's inherently alpha focused, if you do it right. The platforms that are already focused on alpha are going to continue to have an advantage. It's like actually going to be a win that fills their sail up. On the other side, the stuff that's all beta is really going to struggle.

---

**Q: Do you think AI will be like the internet where the entities that control the end user relationships accrue the most value, or if you think it'll be more like the cloud where the infrastructure layer accrues the most value or has the highest margins?**

A: If you thought about the stack as like chips, models, AI infrastructure, AI applications, what I see happening empirically is the models are being commoditized and always under pressure. So the model companies are expanding up. Sometimes they almost call it in a diminutive way a harness, but it's actually they're building software around it that is AI infrastructure to do something like code. Then the people who started as narrow vertical AI solutions are kind of earning their way down the stack to realize like, oh, I need this actual AI infrastructure to be able to scale to my customer base and handle more use cases. So our theory has always been the value is going to accrue in two places at the chips layer and at the AI infrastructure layer, what we would call ontology. Those two layers, I think, are going to be pretty defensible.

---

**Q: What's your sort of mental model for what AI is going to do to the economy in terms of the productivity stats and GDP growth, but then also the job market?**

A: What always irks me about how we talk about AI is as if somehow we have no human agency. AI is going to do X. No, that's not right. Humans are going to use AI to do X. There's a choice here. Do we want to invest in AI slop? What is our normative view of why AI is valuable? How does it result in American prosperity? How does it make our society better, not worse? Restoring the fact that we have agency and therefore an obligation to steer this in a specific way. We have a historic opportunity to fix the fundamental breakdown that happened in the 70s between wage growth and GDP growth. There is an opportunity to give the American worker superpowers with AI. It's David's slingshot in a world where the Chinese Goliath has been this giant sucking sound of American prosperity. If we do that, it's a basis for underwriting the reindustrialization of the country.

---

**Q: What was the real impetus from your research that sort of led to this sort of divorce between production and engineering?**

A: Europe has created exactly zero companies from scratch in the last 50 years worth more than 100 billion euro. We have created all of our trillion dollar companies from scratch in America in the last 50 years. The difference is founders. We kind of had the Europeanization of our mega cap companies until recently. Intel at some point, there was this fork in the road where they could have promoted their CFO to be the CEO or Pat Gelsinger as CTO. Who did they pick? They picked the CFO, the person that Wall Street would understand, not the person who could actually determine the future roadmap. When was the last Boeing CEO to be an engineer? Elon says the pathway to the CEO is through the CTO, which sounds like a crazy, heretical statement.

---

**Q: How do you think about the jobs that tech companies leverage with AI, how you're using it in Palantir?**

A: I'm not sure why the goal is replacing people to begin with. Like isn't the goal to win? Isn't the goal to be dominant in your industry? You want to be better. Maybe being better is about a mixed mammal AI teaming. It's, how do I build the Ironman suit for the salespeople I do have? How do I make the best salespeople more productive and systematized? What is it that makes them good for everyone else? There's all sorts of other ways of thinking about the problem if your goal is winning. But if your goal is AGI, it's the aesthetic of the fact that you couldn't replace the person with this model is offensive, and you're just going to keep driving at that.

---

**Q: How do we win the AI race, particularly as it moves towards more physical AI and robotics, etc.?**

A: I think our biggest risk as a country is suicide, not homicide. I think our problem is actually one of national will and focus. Are we actually addressing the problems that we face here? Are we encouraging the agency and our people to believe the world can be better? This manifests in a sense of just kind of like nihilism and polarization, that we forget what makes us, what unites us, and we focus on what divides us. There's this kind of sense like, hey, Nothing really works. It doesn't really matter. So let's just burn it all down. The idea that somehow the American people are not capable of this thing. How is that a beggar's belief, right? So I think it's actually about will and motivation and leadership.

**Q: Why did you start a film production company?**

A: It really starts with my own assimilation journey. I came to the U.S. as a young child. I settled in Orlando. My assimilation journey as a four-year-old, five-year-old was watching movies with dad on the couch. What were the movies of the 80s and 90s? It was Hunt for Red October, Red Dawn, Rambo 2 and 3. I like to say as a five-year-old, I knew what it felt like to be an American before I knew civics. Even subtle things like I heard from the guy who made the movie 300. That after 300 came out, Navy SEAL recruitment went through the roof. If our entertainment is all Terminator, AI ruins the world, technology is a force of evil. It's all dystopic future scenarios. That sets a sort of condition, which I would juxtapose to my youth in Orlando growing up in the shadow of the Space Coast, which was just like science and technology is amazing.

Like Jack Warner, in the 30s, Germany was the third largest export market for American entertainment. The Nazis actually deployed sensors into Hollywood to control what was being made. Every studio capitulated except for Warner Brothers. Jack Warner is the only person willing to stand up and speak truth. Only a founder can do that. If you're a professional CEO who's employed, you can't survive that. In some ways there's a mirror to the present day Hollywood and the defense industrial base. It's conformity. It's kind of lack of opinion. It's lack of a normative view of what is it trying to communicate.

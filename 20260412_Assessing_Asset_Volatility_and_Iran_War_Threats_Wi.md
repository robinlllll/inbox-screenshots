# Summary

Mike Pyle, Deputy Head of BlackRock's Portfolio Management Group, shares insights on navigating markets amidst geopolitical uncertainty and policy shifts. Pyle emphasizes the U.S. economy's resilience, particularly its insulation from global energy shocks due to its robust natural gas supply. He highlights the shift from a demand-driven to a supply-driven world, influenced by events like the war in Iran and advancements in AI, which require investors to rethink portfolio construction and diversification strategies. Pyle also touches on the increasing importance of energy security, strategic stockpiling, and diversified energy mixes for countries and companies. He notes AI's growing political and policy implications, advising investors to consider these factors when assessing its impact and value accrual.

# Takeaways

* In the face of a large economic shock impacting household and business balance sheets, governments must act swiftly and substantially to prevent long-term labor market and economic damage.
* Policymaking involves shaping the world as desired, while investing requires understanding the world as it currently exists, and confusing the two can lead to poor decisions.
* Diversification is harder to achieve today than in the past due to concentrated equity markets and the unreliable hedging role of government bonds.
* The U.S. economy is relatively insulated from global energy shocks due to its natural gas market, which is not globally integrated.
* The 2020s represent a shift from a demand-driven to a supply-driven world, requiring investors to adapt their strategies to account for logistical interruptions and scarcity.
* Hedge funds and liquid alternative strategies offer diversification by providing exposures that are neutral to broad markets.
* Systematic investors generate alpha through high-breadth insights applicable to a wide range of stocks, focusing on statistical likelihoods across the full universe of stocks over time.
* AI is poised to become a significant political and policy issue, adding a new layer of uncertainty for investors to consider.
* Alpha is increasingly the province of scale, where insight comes from risk management, trading, liquidity, and operational backbone.
* Strategic stockpiling of critical inputs and raw materials is becoming more important for countries and companies seeking greater resilience against supply chain disruptions.

# Q & A

**Q: How does your government experience affect how you look at markets and investing, specifically regarding risk, uncertainty, and opportunities?**

A: Investing and policymaking are different exercises. Policymaking attempts to make the world as you want it to be, or as elected representatives want it. Investing takes the world as it is and makes sound choices about how to invest client capital. The framework I use to think about investing comes back to the blocking and tackling of active management. It's about making forecasts that are right about the world, having a wide set of those forecasts to build a diversified portfolio, and then translating those insights efficiently into the assets you own. I try to keep them distinct because one's about the world as you might hope it to be, and the other is about the world as it is.

---

**Q: How do you manage when systematic signals conflict with the fundamental narrative driving most conversations, and who wins that debate?**

A: We believe in individual portfolio managers and teams empowered to make judgments they are accountable for. It's okay if systematic investors have different views than our fundamental teams. What systematic investors do is different from what fundamental investors do. Our fundamental investors harness all potential sources of insight to deeply understand an individual company and its likelihood of outperforming. Systematic investors think about high breadth insights that apply to a wide range of stocks. Where these things can be complementary is in diversification and portfolio construction.

---

**Q: What sort of trends are you observing either in hedge funds or liquid alts, and what kind of strategies are resonating with investors?**

A: What's resonating is the availability of diversification, diversifying the diversifiers beyond just stocks and bonds. The macroeconomic underpinnings of markets have become unmoored in recent years. It is a less predictable framework, whether it's around trends on growth of inflation, trends around monetary and fiscal policy frameworks, the geopolitical environment and the like. Hedge funds and liquid alternative strategies provide tools that allow managers to navigate that environment. Systematic strategies and macro strategies are best poised to offer that different type of return and diversification.

---

**Q: Given all this geopolitical turmoil and market volatility and uncertainty, what do you think investors are not thinking about or talking about but perhaps should be?**

A: While we've been talking about AI, I think that the investment implications of AI have huge uncertainty bands around them. One piece that I think is being underappreciated is the degree to which I think AI is going to become a first-order political and policy issue in the quarters and couple of years ahead. We're seeing the beginnings of that. I think an important dimension of what it's going to mean to invest in AI is understanding that this is going to become a risingly important political and policy issue and an additional dimension of uncertainty that investors are going to have to confront as we make choices around where impact is gonna be felt and values gonna accrue.

---

**Q: The war with Iran and the Strait of Hormuz is creating a new energy supply shock. Tell us your views on this.**

A: The BlackRock Investment Institute did a piece of work back in 2022 called A World Shaped by Supply. The 2010s were defined by aggregate demand. The story post-COVID is not that. It's a world shaped by supply. And I would sort of draw attention to really two episodes that we've seen already in 2026 that highlight this point. One and most obviously is what we've seen in markets since the beginning of the military intervention in Iran and the world pricing to a greater or lesser extent, a pretty traditional negative energy supply shock, higher inflation expectations, lower growth expectations, A pullback in risk really across different types of asset classes, but if you roll the clock back just a couple of weeks before the beginning of hostilities in Iran, you saw a market priced for a different type of supply shock, a positive technology supply shock from AI.

---

**Q: Does this decade require us to fundamentally rethink how we build portfolios, how we manage risk, and how different are the 2020s from the 2010s?**

A: Diversification is an extraordinarily important tool as an investor, and diversification is harder to come by today than it was in the 2010s and has been historically. That's true around the role that government bonds can be relied upon to play in portfolios. It's also true in terms of equity markets and how concentrated equity markets, especially in the United States, have become. Building portfolios means building portfolios that achieve diversification in a world where diversification is less available than it has been in the past through straightforward means like balance 60-40 portfolios. Investors of all types are going to need to reach for private assets, hedge funds and liquid alternative strategies can play in public markets.

---

**Q: What do you think policymakers got right during the great financial crisis recovery, and what was the biggest mistake?**

A: One of the principal lessons coming out of the global financial crisis is that in the face of a large economic shock, a shock that impacts the balance sheets of households and businesses, the government needs to act speedily and with size to prevent the labor market damage, the economic damage from being an overhang that lasts for a long time. One of the things that a lot of policymakers concluded coming out of the GFC is we just didn't do enough quickly enough. As a result, we had a very slow recovery that didn't last just a couple of years, but 10, 12 years and had labor market damage that lasted for longer than it needed to, because we didn't ask with the force and speed that we needed to.

---

**Q: You were the president's personal envoy to groups like the G7, the G20, APEC summits. How effective are these global organizations?**

A: President Biden started from the place of believing that the United States acts with greatest impact in the world when it acts alongside our closest allies and partners. That's part of the reason why the G7 during the years I was serving was perhaps at the height of its impact and influence across time. After Russia's invasion of Ukraine, really acting with force with one voice, not just as the United States, but as a set of allies, to put historic A set of sanctions on Russia to put historic economic pressure on Russia and to do that in a way that made sure that it wasn't just the United States acting, but all of our allies and partners together around the world acting in concert, delivering a stronger force of policy than the United States for all of its power and might could have delivered by itself.

**Q: What is the BlackRock Investment Institute, and who consumes the output of this?**

A: I think one of our observations about The asset management industry, the hedge fund industry over the last 10 or 15 years is that 10 or 15 years ago, people viewed hedge fund alpha, alpha more broadly perhaps as the province of small niche players who understood some corner of the market deeper and better than anybody else. I think 10 or 15 years on, I think we've come to see that alpha is more the province of scale. The purpose of the BlackRock Investment Institute is one, to inform those health research discussions to really inform and drive the investment debate within the firm, but then also to open up the curtain and let our clients see and consume a lot of the research that our portfolio managers are using day in and day out to inform their own thinking, their own investment decision-making.

**Q: What are some of the more durable economic themes going to be coming out of the energy shock?**

A: I think energy security, which I think post-COVID, post-Russia's invasion was already front of mind for countries, companies, economies around the world, is only going to become more so. Secondly, I think what we're going to see both from countries and from companies is increased focus on strategic stockpiling. You're going to see companies and countries really turn to using resources to build stockpiles of those critical inputs. We've talked for a long time about the ways in which There's been a turn in the world from just-in-time supply chains to resilient supply chains. And then I do think that countries and companies around the world are going to be looking At their energy mix.

There's just the human being sitting in front of you and you've got to do your role to support them in the way you can.

What I expected to learn when I left government the first time was how do I do deep macroeconomic research. What I hadn't appreciated and came to really love learning about was, okay, how do you actually take five or six or seven of those insights? Put them in a portfolio, understand how much return each can generate, understand how they're correlated, how they move with one another, and then build a portfolio of those insights that is going to deliver the right risk, the right return for clients.

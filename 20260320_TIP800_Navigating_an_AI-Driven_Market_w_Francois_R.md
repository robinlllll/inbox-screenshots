# Summary

François Rochon discusses key lessons from 2025, particularly regarding artificial intelligence and its impact on the market. He views AI as a revolution comparable to the early internet, cautioning against over-optimistic valuations due to circular investment dynamics involving companies like Nvidia, OpenAI, and Oracle. Rochon highlights how Alphabet and Meta are strategically using massive CapEx spending to defend and grow their businesses amid the AI boom. He also addresses the significant drop in Constellation Software's stock price, attributing it to AI-related fears and the departure of CEO Mark Leonard, while still believing the shares are cheap. Essential qualities for long-term investing success are rationality, humility, and patience.

# Takeaways

* AI's rapid advancement and the swift changes in industry leadership make it difficult for companies to establish lasting moats in the AI space.
* The potential for low returns on AI investments is a drawback for companies like Alphabet and Meta, even though they can fund these investments without borrowing.
* Meta's AI investments have enabled better ad targeting, resulting in higher ad prices and revenue, demonstrating a tangible return on investment in AI.
* The market's repricing of software company valuations reflects concerns about AI's potential to disrupt the software industry, leading to lower P/E ratios.
* Constellation Software's potential to acquire companies at more attractive prices due to the software sell-off could lead to better returns going forward.
* A key to long-term investing success involves rationality, humility, and patience.
* The most effective way to maintain patience in the stock market is to focus on the underlying performance and fundamentals of the company rather than market sentiment.
* The exceptional performance of the S&P 500 over the past decade was driven by the extraordinary growth of a few dominant companies, which may not be sustainable long-term.
* The strength and resilience of a brand, such as Louis Vuitton, are critical for long-term success in the luxury goods market, outweighing short-term economic cycles.
* China's increasing GDP per capita will likely drive greater demand for luxury goods, making it a strategically important market for companies like LVMH despite current challenges.

# Q & A

**Q: What are the three essential qualities for success in the stock market, and why do investors often fall short in these areas?**

A: The three essential qualities are rationality, humility, and patience. Rationality involves looking at reality objectively, setting aside emotions, and focusing on facts, which gives you a fantastic advantage because most investors aren't able to do it. Humility is essential for continuous improvement; you cannot improve if you think you know everything. Finally, patience is learned through experience; it sometimes takes years to be rewarded for an investment. Focus on what's happening with the company and remember that genius in the stock market is really disguised patience.

---

**Q: Given the circular nature of AI investments, where companies like Nvidia invest in OpenAI, which then contracts with Oracle, how should investors value the profits of these companies, considering the contracts may only last three to five years?**

A: If Nvidia invests $100 billion in OpenAI, and OpenAI gives a $300 billion contract to Oracle, who then buys $40 billion of Nvidia chips over three years, resulting in $7 billion of profit per year for Nvidia, the key question is how recurring is this $7 billion? If you're a shareholder of Nvidia, you have to look at that. The market might give a P/E of 25 times, capitalizing this $7 billion over 25 years, resulting in a $175 billion valuation. However, if the $7 billion is not recurring because Nvidia indirectly finances its own sales, you should capitalize it over just three years, reflecting that it's not a recurring revenue.

---

**Q: Constellation Software's stock fell by more than 50% from its all-time high; how has your investment thesis changed, if at all, in light of Mark Leonard stepping down as CEO and fears related to disruption from AI?**

A: I invested in Constellation because I thought Mark Leonard was a fantastic CEO. When he stepped down, that was quite worrisome, but Mark Miller, who took the job, has been with the company since almost day one and is probably the best person to take on the succession. The AI threat is a deeper reason for the stock decline, but I believe the type of service and software that Constellation sells will not be too affected by AI. The market has reevaluated the P/E ratio for software companies, but now you can buy a 20% grower at 18 times earnings, which is cheap. If we're right about our assessment that the business is intact, I do believe that the stock is cheap.

---

**Q: Given that Alphabet and Meta are investing massive amounts in AI infrastructure, how do you think about how these investments could play out for them going forward, especially considering the history of overinvestment in emerging technologies?**

A: Alphabet and Meta have the money, so even if they have a very low return on that investment, it doesn't put the company in jeopardy. I see those investments not as offensive as defensive. I think they are investing those large amounts of money to protect their business, mostly. When ChatGPT was released, it was a valid threat to Google's search business. For Meta, investments in AI have helped them better target ads after Apple changed privacy settings. Meta is probably the company that has gotten the best return on investing in AI because it really helped selling ads.

---

**Q: CarMax and Pfizer were two stocks in your portfolio that fell sharply in 2025; walk us through how you came to the decision that the thesis was broken and it was time to move on from the position.**

A: In terms of CarMax, the market became more competitive, margins went down, and the moat was not as strong as it was 10 years ago. In the case of Pfizer, the CEO left, and results started to be a little disappointing. The company has a little bit of debt, and I'm very wary of debt in general. My threshold is five times net income, and I decided that I didn't want to live with that risk.

---

**Q: How do you handle client or partner relationships during difficult times when your view might differ significantly from what the stock market is telling us?**

A: I manage my own portfolio, and I invest in what I believe are the best 25 names with a long-term view. I buy for the partners and the Giverny Capital clients the same securities as I buy for myself. If I don't buy it for myself, I won't buy it for you. I won't sell it because some of my clients or partners are not happy about it. I've accepted that there'll be ups and downs in my approach. I try to recognize mistakes as soon as possible. By focusing on what's happening to the company, I can probably have more perspectives and be less affected by what the market is saying about the companies I own.

---

**Q: You mentioned that Mark Leonard was your favorite CEO of the companies you owned; what was it that you admired in him and the way he communicated with shareholders and built this phenomenal business over the past 30 years?**

A: He's wise, kind, and thinks of others before him. He's not like the typical business leader with strong ambitions and testosterone. He looks like someone that was in the ZZ Top rock band, but he was like an artist, very thoughtful and sensitive. At some point, he said that I don't want any salary, no bonuses, no stock options. He's very generous, always thinking long term, and always thinking for the good of the business and the shareholder.

---

**Q: In learning more about the developments that are happening in AI, do people generally seem to think of AI as a headwind or an opportunity for software companies?**

A: It is a big thing; it is a big revolution. Anyone that takes it lightly is probably missing something because it is important. You have to adapt, bring AI to your business, and use it intelligently because if your competitors do it, you'll be handicapped if you don't. Constellation is also aware of the threat, and they're taking it very seriously. They can use AI to improve their software and improve the efficiency that they sell those software. Also, Constellation is growing through acquisitions, and I think going the next few years, acquisitions will be much cheaper than they were five years ago.

---

**Q: Your portfolio outperformed the S&P over the first two decades and then underperformed over the most recent decade; what does this tell us about where the S&P might go in the decade that lies ahead?**

A: The last decade has been an extraordinary period for the S&P 500. The earnings have done very well also, but 9% is higher than historical numbers for the S&P 500. These extraordinary companies—Amazon, Nvidia, Alphabet, Meta, Microsoft—have grown at an extraordinary rate for companies their size. As they came to be a bigger weight in the S&P, their performance increased the overall growth in earnings of the S&P. I would keep my expectation low for the S&P 500 for the next five or 10 years.

---

**Q: Do you see similarities between Kenzale Capital and Progressive, given your long history of following Progressive?**

A: It's not exactly in the same business, but probably they have some of the qualities. One is the adoption of technology to help them improve their underwriting ratio. Progressive was very early using antithematics to better measure the risk of drivers, and Kingsell has been very keen on using technology to have strong software and strong advantages. The consequence of that is that they have a better underwriting ratio, meaning they have better margins of profits. So higher return on equity and they probably can write more insurance because their cost ratio is lower than competitors.

---

**Q: Why is now the time to enter LVMH?**

A: I try to assess what's the earning power and what kind of earnings will the company have in five years. After or during the pandemic, there was a very strong demand for Louis Vuitton products. Almost all the luxury products were very much in demand. The last two years has been much tougher. It's just the nature of the luxury market that there'll be ups and downs. I think the years 21, 22, 23 were very, very strong, but at some point, consumers needed a little pause in their purchasing. I think the key factor is that you want the brand to be as strong as it's ever been. They've got many great brands, not just Louis Vuitton. I think at some point, earnings will rebound.

---

**Q: What is your take on LVMH having exposure in China?**

A: I think long term, it's going to be a positive thing for the simple reason it's not going to be a straight line. I think the Chinese people going forward will continue to increase their wealth, their GDP per capita, just because they're starting from a lower point than, let's say, France or Germany or the United States. I think at some point, it's going to be a real capitalist society. Even in a communist or semi-communist state as they are, they will continue to improve their GDP per capita. As they get richer at a faster rate than North Americans, I think they'll want to acquire LVMH products, luxury products.

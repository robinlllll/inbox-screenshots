# Summary

Marek Capital's Matt Cherwin discusses his firm's investment philosophy, emphasizing the importance of understanding the interplay between money, capital, credit, liquidity, and regulation in driving economies and markets. Cherwin reflects on his career, including lessons learned from the 2008 financial crisis and the 2019 repo crisis, which shaped his perspective on risk management. He highlights the potential for mispricing in novel areas like the AI CapEx boom and trophy commercial real estate, where market perceptions may lag behind fundamental realities. Cherwin also touches on the evolving regulatory landscape and the potential impact of a new Federal Reserve leadership on market dynamics.

# Takeaways

* Understanding how the financial system truly operates requires seeing it from the "engine room," not just as the "captain of the ship."
* The financial system is disaggregating into distinct buckets: systemically important banks, new GSIBs (private credit firms), and traders, effectively recreating Glass-Steagall.
* Money represents how it moves through the system, while capital is how much exists.
* A business-forward administration that needs rates to come down can significantly influence market dynamics.
* The intertwining of risk-free rates and credit spreads means they no longer exist separately.
* Managing risk effectively involves understanding return and risk attribution and knowing the exits.
* Investors often underestimate the power of financial engineering to recover and create value when financing conditions are favorable.
* The market's overreaction to regulatory changes can create opportunities for those who understand the true impact.
* A focus on liabilities over assets provides a better understanding of financial stability.
* The most successful investment opportunities arise when an asset's fundamentals have improved, but its price has not yet reflected that change.

# Q & A

**Q: What problem does Marek Capital solve that couldn't be solved at a large Wall Street bank?**

A: Quite simply, there are some things that banks can do and some things they can't, and some things they can do but don't want to. I've always been involved in markets like rates, mortgages, securitized products, and corporate credit. I've always felt that when you can apply various lenses—the trader, the structure, the operator—you understand it better. When you learn about the financial system it sits within, you can take advantage of the risk and return in a more efficient way.

---

**Q: How did your experiences at JP Morgan Chase change how you perceive risk?**

A: I spent 20-odd years trading mortgages, rates, corporate credit, and working with specialty finance companies. In late 2019, I moved to be the CIO and Treasurer, running the firm's capital and managing structural risk. Then the repo crisis hit, followed by the pandemic. I saw how the financial system really works and how it applies to everything I'd done. I went from being a captain of the ship to working in the engine room, seeing the gearing and how it works, what could stop it from working. It was an "aha" moment, like getting new glasses and finally seeing clearly.

---

**Q: What are the key elements of Marek Capital's investment philosophy?**

A: I think about markets in terms of money, capital, credit, liquidity, and regulation—MCCLR. Money is how you make it, destroy it, and how it moves. Capital is how much you have, how you measure it. Credit is how it's being formed and moving through the system. We wrote a paper describing the new version of the financial system, where you're de facto recreating Glass-Steagall. You have GSIBs like JPMorgan, Wells, and Bank of America, and the new GSIBs like Apollo, Blackstone, and KKR, who are making credit extension decisions. Traders like Citadel Securities and Jump are also key. We think about where credit comes from, where it goes, who wins, who loses, and what the flywheels are. We believe MCCLR drives economies, markets, and prices, which helps us understand monetary policy, real estate, and consumer behavior.

---

**Q: How is Glass-Steagall being reapplied in today's financial markets?**

A: Glass-Steagall separated FDIC-safe banks from riskier investment banks. Now, there are rules about what you can and can't do, but a lot has morphed into private credit, where credit is extended through different fashions and some rules don't apply. This allows us to trade markets differently or make markets in ways big banks can't. Big banks are viewed as super safe, which has advantages. We see and understand these artificial boundaries and put them into our portfolio construction. It's all up for grabs again, with potential changes coming from new Fed leadership.

---

**Q: Is the current opportunity set in the market greater than what we typically see?**

A: We have an administration that's in the business of being in business. We also need rates to come down significantly due to the country's debt. We construct our portfolio with an investment thesis and narrative, ensuring everything fits and contributes to our goals or protects us from potential downsides. We think it's a very business-forward environment that needs rates to come down. We'll have a new Fed chair in the middle of June, which could be a catalyst for change. We also see a bias in markets to strip back layers of bureaucracy. This combination is spinning flywheels in the market, and people are underestimating their power.

---

**Q: How do you define and manage risk at Marek Capital, especially considering the market changes you observed in 2019?**

A: Managing risk at scale is a skill. Numbers like VAR and stress tests are starting points for a conversation. I prefer a stress-based framework with simple scenarios like rates going up or down, a credit crunch, or a flight to quality. We also have scenarios like "QE forever and ever." It's about understanding where the risk is coming from, the return attribution, and, importantly, what could go wrong. It's a philosophy to ensure we're pointed towards our goals, managing risk properly, and knowing what we'd do if things changed.

---

**Q: How does Marek Capital use artificial intelligence in its operations?**

A: We use AI every day to build tools more quickly than ever before. Our team can build tools in a week that would have taken a year elsewhere. AI is also interesting for risk management, compliance, and KYC. We look at the AI CapEx boom and see commercial real estate with securitization technology around it. We consider where it is, if it's built, who the tenants are, and what it's worth. This is a source of very cheap risk for us to look at, and we're opinionated about which ones we like.

---

**Q: What is Marek Capital's structure? Are you an alt credit shop, a multi-strat shop, or a hybrid?**

A: We are who we are, and we do it the way we do it. Right now, we're running a hedge fund that trades rates, mortgages, securitized products, corporate credit, and related equities as tools in the toolbox. We do it in one collaborative portfolio. We have specialists in rates, mortgages, ABS, credit, and CLOs. I'm on the phone every day with traders and salespeople. We trade it as one book.

---

**Q: What is drawing your attention in structured credit, corporates, and relative value today?**

A: We really think one of the places to extract from the flywheel is in securitized markets. As an example, we've been very focused on trophy quality office in gateway cities. This came from us pulling at the thread of how the financial system works. The new GSIBs—Apollo, Ares, KKR, Blackstone, BlackRock—were coming back to the office and growing, needing nice offices. There's not that much trophy real estate out there, leading us to have strong conviction about a supply-demand imbalance in commercial real estate when applied correctly. We look for what's the best place.

---

**Q: What do you think investors in the credit and alternative investment space are overlooking?**

A: They're underestimating the power of the flywheel. With my background, I've seen a lot of things blow up, but I think they're underestimating the things that could go right, the power of financing, and the mechanics around financing and the provision of liquidity and credit spreads. When they're good and when they're tight and when the machine is flowing, what that financial engineering can really do to both recover value and create value. Also, if Kevin Warsh ends up as Fed chair, he could change everything, believing we'll have a big productivity dividend from AI and deregulation.

---

**Q: What's the elevator pitch for Marek Capital, and what problem does it solve that a large Wall Street bank can't?**

A: I saw some interesting stuff, I learned some interesting stuff, and there's an opportunity set that we want to prosecute right now. It is an incredible time to do so. We built a team, and I said, let me just—here's how I think about markets. I think about it in terms of money, capital, credit, liquidity, and regulation. That's my five.

---

**Q: What advice would you give to a college grad interested in a career in investing, credit, or trading?**

A: It doesn't have to be a commitment for life. Look at it as what's something I'm interested in being interested in. Pick the kind of people you want to work with and be around good people who will teach you, who will support what you're doing. Just say, I'm going to give this a spin for three to five years. If I like it, I love it, maybe I'll sign up for another five. You have an opportunity to try something out and see if it's for you.

---

**Q: What do you know about the world of trading credit and investing in alternative sources of liquidity now that would have been helpful early in your career?**

A: I wish I knew a fraction of what we are applying at Marek any point before we did this. If I knew a drop of what we're doing when I sat in other seats, yeah, I'll put that all in the I wish I knew bucket.

|Glass-Steagall Act|A U.S. law passed in 1933 that separated commercial banking (handling deposits) from investment banking (underwriting and trading securities). Its partial repeal in the late 1990s led to the consolidation of financial institutions and is discussed in the context of how the financial system is evolving.|

# Summary

Nick Turley from OpenAI discusses the evolution and future of ChatGPT with Apoorv Agrawal. Originally intended as a free demo, ChatGPT's viral success led to a subscription model to manage demand and ensure stability. Turley emphasizes the goal of building a "super assistant" that helps users achieve diverse goals, highlighting the importance of long-term retention as a key metric. He attributes ChatGPT's growth to friction removal, core product investments like search and personalization, and continuous model improvements. Looking ahead, Turley envisions ChatGPT becoming more proactive and capable of taking actions, moving beyond a chatbot interface to deliver tangible outcomes and integrate AI more seamlessly into users' lives.

# Takeaways

* ChatGPT's initial freemium model was an accident born of necessity, as subscriptions were implemented to manage demand for a demo that unexpectedly went viral.
* Durable value in AI products is indicated by long-term user retention, which should be prioritized over direct revenue generation.
* The key to building a retentive AI product lies in the accumulation of many small improvements that systematically enhance the user experience.
* ChatGPT's growth can be attributed to a combination of friction removal, core product investments (like search and personalization), and iterative model improvements.
* To reach the next billion users, AI products need to evolve beyond being raw "computer terminals" and offer more intuitive, proactive assistance.
* The most promising path for AI agents involves domain-specific applications, where testability and immediate feedback facilitate rapid improvement.
* Chat, while valuable for expressing intent, is not always the ideal modality for AI output, which should often take the form of a deliverable artifact.
* Power users are invaluable for product discovery in AI, as their experimentation reveals possibilities that developers might not anticipate.
* The primary goal of OpenAI's ad pilots is to broaden access to advanced AI technology, not merely to generate revenue.
* A critical tradeoff in AI development is balancing incremental improvements to existing use cases with the productization of breakthrough technologies.

# Q & A

**Q: How do we get the next billion users, and how will ChatGPT evolve beyond being just a chatbot?**

A: To reach the next billion users, we need to make the product more intuitive and proactive. Currently, ChatGPT is like a raw appliance; users need to discover its uses themselves. We need to frame its intelligence to solve users' specific problems, making it more like an operating system of software. Being proactive is also crucial, helping users who are too busy to delegate tasks to AI. We're also excited about productizing our next-generation reasoning models, so they work on users' behalf without them even knowing. This technology will evolve beyond pure chatbots pretty fast.

---

**Q: What drove ChatGPT's historical growth and success to reach 900 million weekly active users?**

A: Our growth has been roughly one-third from classic friction removal, one-third from core product investments, and one-third from pure model improvements. Removing the authentication wall was a huge moment. Core product investments like search and personalization, developed jointly by research and product teams, have also been critical. Model improvements, both major step changes like GPT-4 and smaller iterations based on user feedback, have significantly contributed to our retention as well.

---

**Q: Given that AI consumer markets tend to be winner-take-most, what levers got ChatGPT to scale, considering Meta and Google have massive distribution?**

A: Distribution was not enough. Looking back, about a third of our success came from classic friction removal, like removing the login requirement, which Sam Altman had suggested from day one. Another third came from core product investments, especially collaborations between research and product teams on features like search and personalization. The final third is from model improvements, including both major releases like GPT-4 and smaller, iterative updates based on user feedback.

---

**Q: How are you thinking about pricing, especially for power users who are getting a lot of value from ChatGPT?**

A: Pricing will significantly evolve as the technology changes. ChatGPT was initially free as a demo, and subscriptions were introduced to manage demand. We then put GPT-4 behind a paywall because we couldn't serve it to everyone for free. Subscriptions felt like the fairest way to provide access. Now, with breakthroughs like test time compute, power users want to use more and more intelligence. Having an unlimited plan might not make sense, similar to unlimited electricity plans. We'll be thoughtful about evolving our plans, given the magnitude of our technical and product breakthroughs.

---

**Q: With Sam Altman's historical reluctance about ads, what changed to make OpenAI pilot ads in ChatGPT?**

A: We've always said that if we were to do ads, we'd have to be really thoughtful about it. We engaged the company to determine how to preserve what's magical about ChatGPT while bringing our most advanced tech to anyone, regardless of their ability to pay. We're very early with our pilots, but I feel really proud of the principles we've established. It's very important that the answers ChatGPT provides are independent and that we respect user privacy.

---

**Q: What's an example of a modality that might take ChatGPT beyond a chatbot?**

A: Chat will always be important, and it’s less about chat and more about natural language, where you can express yourself to the machine naturally, whether that's text, voice, or structured UI rendered by the model. Chat is a great way to express intent, but it’s not a great output. In many cases, what you want back is an artifact, like a trip plan or an analysis. Chat will remain the way you disambiguate intent and kick off tasks, but it won’t necessarily be the final deliverable.

---

**Q: What are the principles that guide your partnerships for ChatGPT?**

A: Partnerships are a great way to bring two products together and expose ChatGPT to new users. The most important thing is the user experience. It needs to be amazing. You can get users to click on things, but if the experience isn't awesome, they'll churn. So, it needs to accrue to the user. We're lucky to have a great brand, and anything we do needs to be accretive to that.

---

**Q: What's a trade-off you're making that people might not appreciate from the outside?**

A: A major trade-off is delivering on existing use cases versus productizing step-change technology that will generate entirely new use cases. ChatGPT was an open-ended product that allowed the world to discover what it could do. Post-launch, we can systematically improve on the things people want to use it for. Balancing making the core product better today with providing access to step-change technology is difficult.

---

**Q: How do you allocate GPUs between ChatGPT, Codex, and research?**

A: We prioritize our existing users first, ensuring a fast and reliable product. For new capabilities, the naive approach would be to look at incremental revenue per GPU. However, we often have breakthrough capabilities that are zero to one. For example, we couldn't have predicted the consumer demand for Deep Research. So, we balance things that are no-brainers with brand-new ideas. On the research side, a big part of Mark's job is figuring out what research to fund, and GPUs are a big part of that.

---

**Q: What is the world underestimating about ChatGPT?**

A: People are increasingly viewing ChatGPT as a true thought partner, not just something that answers questions. It's a sparring partner that helps you think things through, from life advice to work analysis. It's becoming a second brain. As we nail proactivity and tasks, it will feel like a teammate at work and a super assistant at home.

---

**Q: Given the competition in the AI landscape, how would you frame ChatGPT's differentiation?**

A: The biggest differentiation is the team behind it. Anything we build will get copied, so it's important that we evolve the category and build the super assistant we've always imagined. We have an amazing team across research, engineering, and design. Our unique ability has been to bring those functions together to build something at the intersection of useful and possible.

---

**Q: What was the "Code Red" moment like, and what led to it?**

A: Code Reds are a tool we use to create focus. At the end of last year, we felt like we needed to show up for our users and focus on the basics, like reliability, performance, and personalization. It was an opportunity to work with folks I don't normally get to work with on making the product great. We exited the Code Red with the launch of 5.3 and 5.4.

---

**Q: What advice would you have for students in school now, given how fast the world is changing?**

A: The most important perma-skill is curiosity. If the machine can answer all your questions, you better have good questions. The only way to have good questions is to pursue the things you're actually excited about from an early age. If you foster curiosity, you will know how to adapt to an evolving landscape of tools, AIs, and jobs.

---

**Q: What is a job that gets more valuable, not less, as AI gets better?**

A: Writing is very important, not because AI can't write, but because the skill of writing forces you to be very clear on what you have to say. Even though prompt engineering is going away, expressing what you want to a machine requires you to be a pretty good and precise writer. Any profession that involves very clear writing and, therefore, thinking is well set up.

---

**Q: What has been your AGI moment?**

A: A few weeks after I joined OpenAI, GPT-4 had finished training. It didn't impress me at all that week because it didn't work, as we hadn't figured out how to post-train it. Seeing it go from that to an entire step change was profoundly humbling. Two things GPT-4 did felt like AGI to me: it could do poetry, and it could produce code that actually worked. Also, when I realized GPT-4 could simulate an entire computer terminal, it blew my mind.

---

**Q: What are you thinking about ChatGPT as a shopping assistant?**

A: Shopping is one of those use cases that exist organically in ChatGPT today and they work. You can ask ChatGPT about any purchase you might be planning and get pretty excellent advice. But it's also one of those cases where the experience that exists in chat today, it's not the perfect experience that you would want, because shopping is very visual, for example. So you're going to want to actually see products and images and be able to compare and contrast, not just read walls of text.

---

**Q: What is our focus on for power users, casual users, and early users?**

A: I feel accountable to our entire user base, and even our non-users, because products like ChatGPT can have real externalities on all humans. It’s really useful to imagine the extremes. One extreme being a user who doesn’t care about AI at all, who has a busy life and needs to be convinced of the value that we can provide, and the other useful extreme is our power user base because power users are the users who teach us what’s possible. It’s actually impossible for us to do all the product discovery on our own, simply because of how empirical this technology is and how much you actually learn post launch.
